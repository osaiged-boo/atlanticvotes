Byelection bonanza: most MP turnover in decades as six more ridings vote Dec. 7

November 25, 2026

Voters in six ridings go to the polls on December 7, bringing to 13 the number of byelections held since the April 2025 general election, CBC News reports — the most this soon after a vote in more than 75 years.

The last time the count ran higher this quickly was after the 1949 election, when prime minister Louis St. Laurent had called 16 byelections by October 1950. Most of those vacancies came from appointments to the bench or the Senate, and five of the departing MPs had died in office. In Parliament’s earliest days the numbers ran higher still, as MPs resigned and stood again on taking a cabinet post, a practice that ended in 1931.

The departures behind this Parliament’s tally span both sides of the House and a wide range of reasons. Conservative Damien Kurek was the first to go, stepping aside so leader Pierre Poilievre could reclaim a seat. Prime Minister Mark Carney appointed former MPs Bill Blair and Jonathan Wilkinson to diplomatic posts and Richard Martel to the Senate. Four MPs left for provincial or municipal office, Larry Brock returned to his local Crown attorney’s office, and former cabinet ministers Steven Guilbeault and Chrystia Freeland departed after years in the former Trudeau government. Conservative Cathay Wagantall, on the Opposition benches since 2015, also left. And the count may yet grow: Scott Aitchison has signalled he will resign his Parry Sound–Muskoka seat in the coming weeks, which would point to a 14th byelection.

Analysts caution against reading a single cause into the churn. Polls analyst Éric Grenier told CBC the departures could be “just a bit of a coincidence,” given how varied the reasons are. Carleton political science professor Jonathan Malloy pointed to the unexpected April 2025 result, with Liberals who had expected to lose under Justin Trudeau now serving in Carney’s government and Conservatives whose hopes of forming government were dashed, while Grenier noted the Liberals securing a majority earlier in 2026 may have shifted MPs’ thinking from the next 18 months to the next four years.

One of the year’s byelections carries a historical echo of its own. Terrebonne went to a byelection earlier in 2026 after its general-election result was declared void over vote-counting issues, paralleling a 1950 byelection in the Nova Scotia riding of Annapolis–Kings, also voided over vote counting.
