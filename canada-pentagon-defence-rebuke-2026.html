Pentagon tells Canada to ‘get serious’ on defence spending and the F-35 review

November 24, 2026

The Pentagon published an unusually blunt fact sheet on United States-Canada defence relations on Monday, accusing Ottawa of failing to make the tough decisions needed to modernise its military and meet its NATO commitments, and leaving American taxpayers to shoulder the burden of continental defence. “Canada’s defense shortfalls speak for themselves,” it said. “Canada must get serious and focused.”

The fact sheet put numbers to the criticism. In the 2025-26 fiscal year, only 41.9 per cent of Canada’s air force fleet was serviceable and 53.9 per cent of its naval fleet, while the army’s operational readiness stood at 48 per cent.

It also criticised Ottawa’s review of its planned purchase of 88 American-made F-35 fighter jets, warning that a mixed fighter fleet could worsen personnel shortages and accusing Canadian leaders of putting domestic economic interests ahead of military effectiveness, creating uncertainty for NORAD, the joint air defence command. Prime Minister Mark Carney put the purchase under review as President Donald Trump threatened Canadian sovereignty and imposed tariffs, and Canada has examined Sweden’s Saab Gripen as an alternative that could bring manufacturing jobs. Canada is committed to the first 16 F-35s, with the rest under review. Carney said in June that Canada should no longer send three-quarters of its defence capital spending to the United States.

Canada has reached NATO’s previous target of spending 2 per cent of gross domestic product on defence and joined allies pledging to reach 5 per cent by 2035, including related security investments. Its latest budget projects combined defence and security spending of 4.1 per cent of GDP by 2027-28, but only 2.6 per cent to core defence. The Pentagon said there is no credible, fully costed plan to meet the 5 per cent target and that the largest spending increases are postponed until after the 2029 federal election. Canada’s parliamentary budget watchdog has also questioned how the government will meet the target.

The rebuke follows a classified directive from Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth that Republican Senator Mike Rounds said largely restricts American military officials’ participation in Canadian think-tank and social events. Speaking on Saturday at the Halifax International Security Forum, Rounds said the directive was not expected to affect joint military operations or intelligence sharing. The forum’s president, Peter Van Praagh, had warned on Friday that the United States can no longer be counted on as a predictable leader of the democratic world. Canada’s military did not immediately offer a substantive response to the Pentagon’s criticism.
